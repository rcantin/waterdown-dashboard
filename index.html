    Planned corridor north of the village core to redirect east–west traffic (linking toward Hwy 6 / Centre Rd / Parkside area). AI data centres in the community

Large AI data centres need a lot of electricity and often water for cooling. Hamilton has seen proposals (including on former industrial harbour land), public meetings with strong turnout, and debate over a pause while the city studies impacts. Other Ontario municipalities have also slowed or reviewed approvals. Federal policy is pushing more Canadian compute capacity; industry pitches investment and tax base; residents raise grid, rates, noise, water, and job-quality concerns.

Arguments often made in favour

  • Private capital, construction jobs, and ongoing property tax revenue
  • More domestic computing capacity and less reliance on foreign cloud providers
  • On heavy-industrial sites, existing power infrastructure may limit impact on the residential grid
  • Some designs use non-municipal water (e.g. bay water) or closed-loop cooling to cut potable use

Arguments often made against or for caution

  • Very high continuous power demand; risk of pressure on the grid and household rates
  • Water use, noise, heat, and limited permanent local jobs relative to the scale of investment
  • Planning rules designed for lighter industrial uses may not capture full impacts
  • Calls for clearer public consultation and municipal rules before approvals

Ticks in our area

Blacklegged (deer) ticks are established across much of southern Ontario, including the Hamilton region and nearby conservation areas, trails, and grassy or wooded edges. They can carry Lyme disease and other pathogens; American dog ticks are common but do not transmit Lyme. Risk is higher in warmer months when people and pets are outdoors.

Identification: Blacklegged ticks are smaller than dog ticks; adults are dark with black legs. Nymphs are poppy-seed sized and easy to miss. Photos submitted to eTick help confirm species.

If you find an attached tick: Use fine-tipped tweezers, grasp close to the skin, and pull straight out steadily—do not twist, squeeze the body, or use heat or petroleum jelly. Clean the area. Save the tick (e.g. sealed container) or photograph it for identification.

After a bite: Lyme is usually only a risk if a blacklegged tick was attached for many hours (often cited as 24+). Watch for fever, fatigue, muscle/joint aches, or an expanding rash (sometimes bull’s-eye) for about 30 days. Contact a health care provider or pharmacist promptly—Ontario guidance allows assessment for preventive antibiotics in some cases. Not every tick is infected, and early treatment is effective when needed.

Prevention: Long clothing, tuck pants into socks, DEET or other approved repellents, stay on paths, check yourself/kids/pets after outdoor time, and reduce yard tick habitat where practical.
